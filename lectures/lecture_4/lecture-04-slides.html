Part 2 · Grain
“One row = one order.”
The grain is the one-sentence definition of what a single fact row is — decided before you build anything else. Get it wrong (one row per line item, by accident) and every aggregate on top silently means something else.
Source: lecture-04-notes.md, Part 2; Kimball Group, “Grain”
Say Kimball calls declaring the grain 'the pivotal step in a dimensional design': it establishes exactly what a single fact table row represents. Everything else — which dimensions, which measures — has to agree with that sentence. Grain comes back in Part 4 as the fan-out trap.
Part 2 · Build Tricks

dim_date is generated once, not computed every query

CREATE TABLE dim_date AS SELECT d::date AS dateKey, EXTRACT(quarter FROM d)::int AS quarter, EXTRACT(month FROM d)::int AS month, to_char(d, 'Mon') AS monthName, EXTRACT(isodow FROM d)::int AS dayOfWeek, to_char(d, 'Dy') AS dayName FROM generate_series(DATE '2024-01-01', DATE '2024-12-31', INTERVAL '1 day') AS d;
366
rows — every day of 2024, a leap year. Sundays included, even though the shop is closed.
  • Quarter, month, day name: computed once, stored as columns.
  • No query ever works out “what quarter is this date?” again.
Source: lecture-04-demo.sql, Part 6 (CREATE TABLE dim_date → SELECT 366); Kimball Group, “Calendar Date Dimensions”
Say Calendars are cheap to precompute and expensive to keep recomputing. A date dimension lists every day, not only the days something happened — the same idea as the Chair and Levon: a table of events can't show what didn't happen. Kimball: calendar date dimensions are attached to virtually every fact table.
Part 2 · Build Tricks

Make “missing” a value, not an absence

INSERT INTO dim_employee VALUES (0, '(online)', 'Online', '(none)');
and in the fact table build:
coalesce(o.employeeID, 0) AS employeeKey
  • Online orders have no employee: employeeID IS NULL.
  • Row 0 gives them one. Every fact row now has a valid, non-null employee key.
  • Every join to dim_employee is an ordinary join — never a “what if it's NULL” special case.
PayoffPart 6 of the demo never needs LEFT JOIN against dim_employee. Plain inner joins lose nothing.
Source: lecture-04-demo.sql, Part 6; lecture-04-notes.md, Part 2; Kimball Group, “Nulls in Fact Tables”
Say Why this avoids LEFT JOIN: an inner join drops a row only when it can't find a partner. With the unknown member, every fact row's employeeKey (0 for online) matches a real row in dim_employee, so the inner join finds a partner for all 600 and drops nothing. Compare Part 4: the same inner join against the raw employees table keeps 419 of 600. Kimball: nulls must be avoided in fact-table foreign keys; use a default row in the dimension instead.

Star vs. snowflake schema

Two shapes for the same facts. One of them is the default.

Say Both are dimensional models. The only difference is what happens to the dimensions.
Part 3 · Star

Star: one fact, flat dimensions around it

Source: lecture-04-demo.sql, Part 6 (tables and columns); Kimball Group, “Star Schemas and OLAP Cubes”
Say This is exactly what Part 6 of the demo builds: fact_sales in the middle, dim_date / dim_customer / dim_product / dim_employee attached directly, each dimension flat. Read one line aloud: one customer, many fact rows. Every 'by what?' is one join from the fact.
Part 3 · Snowflake

Snowflake: a dimension split into sub-tables

Source: lecture-04-notes.md, Part 3 (the dim_category / dim_brand example); Kimball Group, “Snowflaked Dimensions”
Say Same idea, but dim_product gets normalized into dim_product + dim_category + dim_brand, each joined in turn. The other dimensions stay flat here to show that snowflaking is per dimension. This split is the notes' example — the demo builds only the star.
Part 3 · Comparison

Less redundancy, more joins

StarSnowflake
Dimension shapeone flat table per dimensiona dimension split into sub-tables
Redundancycategory and brand repeated on every product roweach category and brand stored once
Joins per queryone hop: fact → dimensionone more hop per sub-table: fact → product → category
Navigabilityone table per “by what?”analyst must know the chain of tables

Source: lecture-04-notes.md, Part 3; Kimball Group, “Snowflaked Dimensions”; Wikipedia, “Snowflake schema”
Say Snowflaking buys less redundancy and costs more joins and more tables for an analyst to navigate — a bad trade most of the time. Wikipedia's article adds that dimension tables are usually small next to the fact table, so the space saved is often negligible.
Part 3 · The Default
Star by default. Snowflake only as the exception — with a specific, concrete reason.
“We generally encourage you to handle many-to-one hierarchical relationships in a single dimension table rather than snowflaking.” — Kimball Group, Design Tip #105
Source: Kimball Group, Design Tip #105, “Snowflakes, Outriggers, and Bridges” (2008); Kimball Group, “Snowflaked Dimensions”
Say Kimball's reasons: snowflakes are harder for business users to understand and navigate, and can hurt query performance; a flat dimension holds the same information. Partial normalization ('outriggers') is 'acceptable in moderation', but 'the exception rather than the rule'. The notes' example of a reason is a genuinely huge dimension; note that Kimball himself calls the storage savings of snowflaking minimal.
Part 3 · Naming Trap

“Snowflake” means two unrelated things

snowflake schema
  • A shape: a dimensional model whose dimensions are normalized into sub-tables.
  • What this Part is about.
Snowflake Inc.
  • A company (founded 2012) selling a cloud data platform.
  • You can build a star schema in it. The name is a coincidence.
Source: lecture-04-notes.md, Part 3; Wikipedia, “Snowflake Inc.”
Say Say it out loud in class: the schema shape and the product are unrelated. A job ad asking for 'Snowflake experience' means the product, not the schema shape.

JOINs, from one idea

Not six keywords to memorize — one mental model that all six fall out of.

Say Everything in this Part hangs off the next slide. If students keep one sentence from today, it's that one.
Part 4 · The Thesis
A join is: pair every row of A with every row of B, then keep the pairs you want.
Every join type is a variation on two questions: which pairs do we keep, and what do we do with the rows that found no pair?
Source: lecture-04-notes.md, Part 4.0
Say That's genuinely it. It means CROSS JOIN isn't a weird separate thing — it's the whole Cartesian product before you've thrown anything away. We start there.
Part 4 · Step 1 of 3

CROSS JOIN: every row × every row

SELECT count(*) FROM orders CROSS JOIN customers;
18,000
rows = 600 orders × 30 customers. Every possible pairing — most of them nonsense.

PostgreSQL defines A CROSS JOIN B as exactly A INNER JOIN B ON TRUE: a join whose condition can never fail.

Source: lecture-04-demo.sql, Part 3 (18000); PostgreSQL 16 docs, “Table Expressions: Joined Tables” (CROSS JOIN ≡ INNER JOIN ON TRUE; N × M rows)
Say Guess before running it. No order belongs to all 30 customers — this is every possible pairing. That's the point: it's the raw material every other join is carved out of. The grid zooms in on 3 orders × 3 customers; the real product is 600 × 30.

  • Seen first, JOIN … ON looks like a magic combine-tables command.
  • Seen derived from CROSS JOIN + WHERE, it's just filtered pairing.

Source: lecture-04-demo.sql, Part 3; lecture-04-notes.md, Part 4.1; PostgreSQL 16 docs, “Planner/Optimizer” (every plan, same result)
Say ON puts the matching condition next to the table it's about. JOIN alone means INNER JOIN. PostgreSQL's planner picks a nested-loop, merge or hash join by estimated cost, and every plan produces the same result — think the slow way, let the database do the fast one.

Part 4 · Aliasing

Two tables, one column name: ambiguous

SELECT orderID, customerID, firstName FROM orders JOIN customers ON customers.customerID = orders.customerID WHERE orderID = 1001;
ERROR: column reference "customerid" is ambiguous
  • customerID exists in both tables; the engine refuses to guess — even though the values are equal on every joined row.
  • Fix: alias the tables (FROM orders o JOIN customers c) and qualify: o.customerID.
Source: lecture-04-demo.sql, Part 3 (error text from running it); lecture-04-notes.md, Part 4.3
Say Error on purpose — read the message aloud. orderID and firstName exist in only one table, so they're fine. The rule is about names, not values. Habit: in any query with two tables, prefix every column, even the unambiguous ones.
Part 4 · Aliasing

An alias doesn't add a nickname — it replaces the name

SELECT orders.orderID FROM orders o WHERE o.orderID = 1001;
ERROR: invalid reference to FROM-clause entry for table "orders" HINT: Perhaps you meant to reference the table alias "o".

Once you write FROM orders o, the name orders stops existing for the rest of that query. PostgreSQL's hint tells you exactly what happened.

Source: lecture-04-demo.sql, Part 3 (error and hint text from running it); lecture-04-notes.md, Part 4.3
Say Error on purpose. The HINT line is PostgreSQL telling you the fix. Same idea returns in self-joins, where aliases are the only way to tell two copies of one table apart.
Part 4 · Shorthand

USING is shorthand. NATURAL JOIN hides the condition.

SELECT customerID, c.firstName, o.orderID FROM orders o JOIN customers c USING (customerID) WHERE o.orderID = 1001;

USING (customerID) = ON o.customerID = c.customerID, and the column appears once in the output.

SELECT count(*) FROM orders NATURAL JOIN sales;
97
rows — both tables hold the same 600 sales
The join condition should always be visible in the codeNATURAL JOIN joined on every shared column name — orderID and eleven more, deliveryDate and rating among them. NULL = NULL is not true, so rows with a NULL there vanished. And it changes silently the day either table gains a column with a matching name. Never use it in real code.
Source: lecture-04-demo.sql, Part 3 (97); PostgreSQL 16 docs, “Joined Tables” (USING, NATURAL) and “Comparison Functions” (NULL comparisons)
Say Guess before revealing why 97. The two tables share twelve column names; NATURAL JOIN requires all twelve to be equal. deliveryDate is NULL for every in-store order, and a comparison with NULL is never true, so those rows are thrown out. Only online orders that also got a rating survive. The PostgreSQL manual itself calls NATURAL 'considerably more risky' than USING.
Part 4 · Outer Joins

LEFT JOIN: keep every row on the left

SELECT count(*) FROM orders o LEFT JOIN employees e ON e.employeeID = o.employeeID;
600
rows — the 181 online orders survive, with the employee columns NULL

Group by coalesce(e.branch, 'Online') and online revenue lands in a visible “Online” bucket: 52,400.41 of completed revenue, the largest of the four.

Source: lecture-04-demo.sql, Part 4 (600; revenue per branch); PostgreSQL 16 docs, “Joined Tables” (LEFT OUTER JOIN)
Say INNER JOIN, but we refuse to lose rows from the left side. Where there's no match on the right, the row stays and the right-hand columns are NULL. 'Left' just means the table written before the word JOIN. Branch revenue (completed): Online 52,400.41; Yerevan Center 48,113.04; Yerevan Mall 35,934.64; Gyumri 9,487.03.
Part 4 · Outer Joins

RIGHT JOIN: the mirror image

SELECT count(*) FROM orders o RIGHT JOIN customers c ON c.customerID = o.customerID;
602
rows: all 600 orders, plus one NULL-padded row for each of the 2 customers who never ordered

Exactly the same as customers LEFT JOIN orders. Keep the table you care about on the left and you never need RIGHT.

Source: lecture-04-demo.sql, Part 4 (602); PostgreSQL 16 docs, “Joined Tables” (RIGHT OUTER JOIN)
Say A RIGHT JOIN B keeps every row of B, which makes it B LEFT JOIN A. Writing LEFT always is this course's style choice, so every query reads the same way — know RIGHT for when you meet it in someone else's code.

Part 4 · The Most Important Trap

WHERE vs. ON: it depends on when each runs

ONWHERE
Runswhile deciding what matches — before NULL-paddingafter the whole join, NULL-padding included
Inner joinsame resultsame result
Outer joinkeeps the unmatched rowscan delete the NULL-padded rows — the LEFT JOIN becomes an INNER JOIN

A WHERE that mentions the right-hand table after a LEFT JOIN should always make you stop and ask: am I filtering matches, or un-inviting the rows my LEFT JOIN was supposed to protect?

Source: lecture-04-notes.md, Part 4.7; PostgreSQL 16 docs, “Joined Tables” (ON vs. WHERE with outer joins)
Say With inner joins only, it genuinely doesn't matter where a condition lives. With outer joins it matters completely. The PostgreSQL manual says it directly: a restriction in ON is processed before the join, one in WHERE after the join — 'That does not matter with inner joins, but it matters a lot with outer joins.'

Part 4 · The Fan-Out Trap

One customer value, copied onto every order row

  • customers.moneySpent is one number per customer — customer grain.
  • orders has many rows per customer — order grain. The join copies moneySpent onto every one of them: not divided, not split, copied whole.
Source: lecture-04-demo.sql, Part 5; customers.moneySpent and order counts from the lecture data (Aram Vardanyan: 40 orders)
Say Aram placed 40 orders, so his 13,544.72 is summed 40 times. Do that for every customer and you get 4,079,958.38. This is Lecture 3's grain lesson again: after this join, the result has one row per order, not one per customer.
Part 4 · The Fan-Out Trap

Count before you sum

SELECT count(*) AS rows, count(DISTINCT c.customerID) AS customers FROM customers c JOIN orders o ON o.customerID = c.customerID;
600
rows summed
28
real customers with orders
The fix, in general
  • Aggregate the fine-grain table first, then join the result back — one row per customer.
  • Or don't join at all if you already have the summary value: SELECT sum(moneySpent) FROM customers.
Source: lecture-04-demo.sql, Part 5 (600 / 28); lecture-04-notes.md, Part 4.10
Say Every customer's moneySpent got summed once per order they placed, not once in total. 600 rows vs 28 customers is the tell. The two customers with no orders don't appear at all, because this is an inner join.
Part 4 · The Fan-Out Trap
“Is the thing I'm summing at the same grain as the rows I'm joining through?”
If not: am I about to sum the same value multiple times?

Part 4 · Non-Equality Joins

ON isn't limited to =

Part 4 · Bridge
A document is a join you saved.
A denormalized JSON document is the materialized result of a one-to-many join. The relational model keeps the pieces separate and joins on demand; the document model pre-joins once and stores the result. Same information — different point where the join cost gets paid.
Source: lecture-04-notes.md, Part 4.12; lecture-04-demo.sql, Part 5 (jsonb_agg query)
Say The demo builds one JSON document per customer with jsonb_agg: all of Diana's orders nested inside her customer object. Reading it back is fast; keeping it right when Diana moves city is the application's problem.
Part 4 · Losslessness

A runnable proof that nothing was lost

SELECT o.orderID, o.orderDate, o.orderTime, …, p.brand, p.price, p.cost FROM orders o JOIN customers c ON c.customerID = o.customerID JOIN products p ON p.productID = o.productID LEFT JOIN employees e ON e.employeeID = o.employeeID EXCEPT SELECT * FROM sales;
0 rows
in both directions

Rebuild the flat file from the four tables, subtract the original: nothing left over. Swap the two halves: nothing again. That's lossless-join decomposition — as a query you can run, not a theory term.

Source: lecture-04-demo.sql, Part 4 (0 rows); PostgreSQL 16 docs, “Select Lists” (NULLs equal for DISTINCT)
Say The full SELECT lists all 29 columns of sales; the slide abbreviates it. The LEFT JOIN to employees matters, or the web shop is lost. EXCEPT compares whole rows and treats two NULLs as equal (the manual states that rule for DISTINCT; the 0-row result shows EXCEPT does the same).
Part 4 · Myth

GROUP BY a, b = GROUP BY b, a

GROUP BY c.customerID, c.firstName, c.lastName
GROUP BY c.lastName, c.firstName, c.customerID
→ the same groups, the same totals
  • A group is defined by the combination of values in the listed columns. The order you list them in changes nothing.
  • If a total looks wrong, GROUP BY order is not the place to look — check for a fan-out first.
Source: lecture-04-notes.md, Part 4.14; PostgreSQL 16 docs, “Table Expressions: GROUP BY”
Say PostgreSQL's definition: GROUP BY groups together the rows that have the same values in all the columns listed. 'All the columns listed' is a set, not a sequence. (The order of output rows is decided by ORDER BY, not GROUP BY.)
The One Idea to Leave With
NATURAL JOIN 600 → 97 · WHERE vs. ON 13 → 12 · fan-out → 4,079,958.38
None of them threw an error. All three returned a table that looked completely normal. SQL correctness bugs are almost always silent — and an AI assistant will write a plausible, syntactically perfect join whether the logic is right or not.
Source: lecture-04-notes.md, Part 5
Say Nothing turns red. The query runs, returns rows, and is simply wrong. That's the whole point of this lecture.
